How the maths works

Cubic yards = area × depth ÷ 324

That is the shortcut from the Texas A&M AgriLife Extension: square feet times depth in inches, divided by 324, gives cubic yards. It works because one cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, and 27 × 12 = 324.

How deep should mulch be?

Two to three inches is right for most flower and shrub beds — enough to hold moisture and block weeds, thin enough that the soil underneath can still breathe. Four inches is for a deep barrier around trees. More than 4 inches can suffocate roots, so do not overdo it.

Bags or bulk?

One cubic yard is 13.5 bags of 2 cubic feet. For a small bed, bags are easy to carry and store. For anything over about 2 cubic yards, bulk delivery is usually much cheaper per yard — and far less plastic to throw away.

Types of mulch — which one for your climate

Mulch comes in two families. Organic mulch is made from things that were once alive — bark, wood chips, straw, shredded leaves, compost, pine needles. It breaks down over time and feeds the soil, but you have to top it up every year or two. Inorganic mulch never breaks down — gravel, river rock, rubber chips, landscape fabric. It lasts for years but feeds the soil nothing.

Most of the bagged mulch in a garden centre is shredded bark or wood — usually pine, cedar or hardwood bark.

ClimateBest pickWhy
Hot and dryLight-coloured bark or straw, 3 to 4 in deepHolds moisture and keeps roots cool. Gravel can get hot enough to scorch roots.
Wet and humidCoarse bark nuggetsLets air through. Fine mulch stays soggy and breeds mould and slugs.
Cold wintersThick straw or coarse wood chipsInsulates roots and stops the soil heaving as it freezes and thaws.
Windy or steep slopesHeavy wood chips, or gravelLight straw just blows away.

Whatever the climate, keep mulch 2 to 3 inches deep and an inch or two away from plant stems — piled against a trunk it holds moisture and rots the bark.
