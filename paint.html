How the maths works

Wall area is perimeter times height

Walk around the room and add up the wall lengths — that is the perimeter. Multiply by the ceiling height and you have the wall area. A 12 by 12 foot room has a 48 foot perimeter; at 8 feet high that is 384 square feet of wall.

Subtract what you do not paint

Doors and windows are not painted, so take them off: a standard door is 21 square feet, a typical window is 15. It is a small correction, but on a room with three windows it saves you nearly half a gallon.

One gallon covers about 350 square feet per coat

That is the safe planning number for smooth, previously painted drywall. The label may say 400, but that assumes perfect conditions and a professional roller. Textured walls take more — plan for 250 to 300 square feet per gallon — and bare drywall drinks paint, so prime it first.

Two coats, not one

Almost every interior job needs two coats for an even color. The only exception is repainting the same color on paint that is already in good shape. Buy the second coat now — matching the color later is harder than it sounds.

Paint grades — what the extra money buys

Paint comes in three quality grades, and the price gap is bigger than it looks. The dearer paint usually covers better, so it needs fewer coats — which is where it pays for itself.

The difference is what is in the can: premium paint has more titanium dioxide (the white pigment that hides the old colour) and better acrylic binders, so it usually covers in two coats where economy paint takes three or four. On a whole house that can make the dearer paint cheaper per job, not dearer.

Water-based or oil-based?

For about 95% of jobs, water-based latex paint is the right choice — it dries fast, has little smell, and cleans up with soap and water. Look for 100% acrylic on the label for exterior work: it flexes with temperature changes and resists moisture and sun. Oil-based (alkyd) paint is now mostly for trim, cabinets and metal, where its hard finish earns its keep — it smells strong, dries slowly and needs solvents to clean up.

Finishes, from flat to gloss

Flat hides wall bumps best but marks easily and is hard to wash — good for ceilings and low-traffic rooms. Eggshell and satin are the middle ground: washable enough for living rooms, kitchens and hallways. Semi-gloss and high-gloss are the toughest and easiest to clean, so they suit doors, trim and bathrooms — but their shine shows every bump, so the surface underneath has to be smooth.
