How the maths works

Slabs: length × width × thickness, divided by 27

A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet — a cube 3 feet on every side. So a slab 10 feet by 10 feet at 4 inches thick is 10 × 10 × (4 ÷ 12) = 33.33 cubic feet, and 33.33 ÷ 27 = 1.23 cubic yards. The 10% waste allowance takes it to 1.36 cubic yards.

Bags: divide by what one bag makes

Bag yields are printed on the bag and are the same across the big brands: an 80 lb bag makes 0.60 cubic feet (45 bags per yard), a 60 lb bag makes 0.45 cubic feet (60 per yard), and a 40 lb bag makes 0.30 cubic feet (90 per yard). So 36.67 cubic feet ÷ 0.60 = 61.1 — we round up to 62 bags.

Post holes: the cylinder formula

A round hole is a cylinder: π × radius² × depth. A 12-inch hole, 24 inches deep, is π × 0.5² × 2 = 1.57 cubic feet. That is 3 bags of 80 lb mix per hole. The fence calculator works out the concrete for a whole fence line at once.

Types of concrete — and the temperatures they work in

Not all bagged concrete is the same. The mix you pick decides how fast it sets and how strong it ends up.

TypeBest forTemperature notes
General-purpose mixSlabs, footings, drivewaysThe standard all-rounder. Works from 50 to 90°F.
Fast-setting mixFence posts, small repairsSets in 20 to 40 minutes. Above 85°F it can set in under 10 minutes — work fast, or wait for a cool day.
High-strength mix (5,000 PSI)Driveways, heavy loadsSame temperature range, but needs a full 28 days to reach full strength — longer in the cold.
Ready-mix (by truck)Anything over about half a cubic yardThe plant adjusts the mix for hot or cold weather. The cheapest way to pour big.

Temperature matters more than the bag. The sweet spot is 50 to 90°F (10 to 32°C), ideally 65 to 75°F. Below 40°F the chemical reaction slows right down, and if fresh concrete freezes before it has gained strength it can be permanently damaged — keep it above 50°F for the first week, with blankets if you have to. Above 90°F it sets too fast, gets hard to work, and loses strength; above 95°F it is outside the industry standard (ACI 305) and should not be poured. Hot day? Pour early in the morning, keep the mix shaded, and have everything ready before the truck arrives.

Mixing concrete at home — in a wheelbarrow

For small jobs, a wheelbarrow and a hoe beat renting a mixer. One to three 80 lb bags per batch is the working limit — a full 6 cubic foot barrow could hold more, but three bags of wet mix already weigh about 250 lb.

  1. Mix where you pour. A loaded barrow is too heavy to push far.
  2. Measure the water. About 3 quarts (0.75 gallons) per 80 lb bag. Pour about two-thirds of it into the barrow first.
  3. Add the dry mix. Tip the bag in rather than shaking it, to keep the dust down.
  4. Chop and fold with a hoe for 3 to 5 minutes until the colour is even and there are no dry pockets hiding in the corners.
  5. Add the last water a little at a time. Stop when a squeezed handful holds its shape. One extra quart in an 80 lb bag can cost up to 40% of the strength.
  6. Place it straight away. Do not add water once it starts to stiffen. Rinse the barrow before the mix sets.

Wet concrete burns skin — wear gloves and eye protection, and wash off any splashes right away.

Common questions

How much concrete do I need for a 10×10 slab?

1.36 cubic yards with the 10% waste allowance, or 62 bags of 80 lb mix.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete are in a cubic yard?

45 bags.

Can I pour concrete when it is cold?

Below 40°F the chemical reaction slows right down, and fresh concrete can be permanently damaged if it freezes. Do not pour on frozen ground, and keep the concrete above 50°F for the first week.
